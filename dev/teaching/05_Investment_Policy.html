5.1 Introduction

In the previous chapter we introduced a framework for understanding clients. We learned to analyze their stage of life, the source of their wealth, their behavioral biases, and their personality types. With the analytical work being complete, the task shifts from diagnosis to prescription. We must translate this understanding of the financial needs into a concrete investment plan.

An Investment Policy Statement, or IPS, is the document that bridges the gap between client analysis and portfolio construction. It takes everything we have learned about the client and translates it into a clear, written framework that will guide every investment decision made on their behalf. The IPS shows where the portfolio is going and documents the agreement between advisor and client about how to get there.

By the end of this chapter, we will understand not only what an Investment Policy Statement contains but also how to craft one that reflects the client’s complete financial circumstances.

5.2 Firm-Level Investment Policy

An Investment Policy Statement for an individual client does not exist in isolation. It is created within the context of the firm that will implement it, and every investment firm operates according to a set of beliefs, principles, and practices that shape how client portfolios are managed. These may be formally documented in a firm-level investment policy or embedded in the firm’s culture and decision-making processes. Regardless of the format, this firm-level framework establishes the boundaries within which all client-specific IPS documents are developed. It ensures consistency across client relationships and provides the philosophical foundation for the advice the firm delivers.

A firm-level investment policy describes the investment philosophy, strategic approach, and guiding principles that define how the firm manages client assets. While the specific content varies from firm to firm, most investment policies address several core elements.

5.2.1 Investment Philosophy

The investment philosophy expresses the firm’s beliefs about how financial markets work and how investment returns are generated. These beliefs are not abstract. They shape every subsequent decision about portfolio construction and management.

Consider a firm that believes markets are generally efficient. In its view, consistently outperforming through security selection is difficult, so the firm will favor passive investment strategies. Portfolios will be constructed using low-cost index funds and exchange-traded funds that capture broad market returns rather than attempting to beat them. A firm with the opposite conviction, one that believes skilled investors can identify mispriced securities and generate excess returns, will dedicate resources to active management. It will invest in manager research, due diligence, and ongoing monitoring of selected strategies. Between these approaches sits a firm that believes certain factors, such as value, momentum, or quality, have historically delivered premium returns. Such a firm may construct portfolios that tilt systematically toward these characteristics.

Each of these philosophical positions carries direct implications for how client portfolios will be built. A clear philosophy ensures that investment decisions are made consistently across the firm and that clients understand the framework underlying the management of their assets.

5.2.2 Asset Allocation Framework

The asset allocation framework provides guidelines for how client portfolios should be diversified across asset classes. This section identifies the asset classes the firm considers suitable for client portfolios, which typically include global equities, fixed income instruments across the maturity and credit spectrum, real estate, and in some cases commodities or alternative investments.

The framework may also establish strategic allocation ranges that guide how these asset classes are combined for clients with different risk profiles. A conservative client might receive an allocation weighted heavily toward high-quality fixed income with a modest equity component. An aggressive client might hold a portfolio dominated by equities with smaller allocations to diversifying assets. By defining these building blocks and allocation ranges upfront, the framework ensures that all client portfolios, regardless of their specific circumstances, are constructed from a consistent set of components and adhere to the firm’s view of prudent diversification.

5.2.3 Risk Management Principles

Every firm must maintain a clear approach to managing the risks inherent in investing, and the risk management section of the policy translates this responsibility into a coherent framework. It addresses how the firm identifies, measures, and mitigates the various forms of risk that client portfolios face, moving from broad categories to specific, actionable limits.

Diversification across asset classes and geographic regions is the primary defense against market risk, the possibility that broad market movements will erode portfolio value. By spreading exposures, the firm reduces the likelihood that a decline in any single market will inflict lasting damage on client portfolios. Credit risk, which arises when bond issuers default on their obligations, requires a different set of controls. The policy establishes minimum quality standards for fixed income holdings and caps the concentration of exposure to any single issuer, ensuring that no individual default can meaningfully impair a portfolio. Liquidity risk is addressed by restricting the proportion of client assets allocated to illiquid holdings and by maintaining adequate cash reserves. This protects against the scenario in which a client needs to raise cash but finds that assets cannot be sold without accepting a steep discount. Operational risk, rooted in failed processes, systems, or human error, is managed through documented procedures, segregation of duties, and regular testing of internal controls.

These broad principles are reinforced through specific requirements that make them enforceable. The policy sets explicit limits on exposure to any single security, sector, or issuer. It provides guidelines for the use of derivatives and other instruments that may be employed for hedging or risk reduction. It also establishes the procedures for ongoing risk monitoring and defines the protocols that govern how the firm responds when limits are breached or when market conditions shift materially. What begins as a statement of principle becomes, through these mechanisms, a practical operating framework for protecting client assets.

5.2.4 Ethical and ESG Considerations

Many firms now incorporate environmental, social, and governance considerations into their investment policies. Negative screening excludes companies or industries that conflict with the firm’s stated values. Positive screening actively seeks companies with strong ESG practices. Full integration considers ESG factors alongside traditional financial metrics in the investment analysis process. The policy may also address any firm-level commitments to responsible investing, such as adherence to established principles or participation in industry initiatives.

By defining its stance on these issues, the firm provides guidance for advisors who must address client questions about the alignment of their portfolios with personal values.

5.2.5 The Function of a Firm-Level Policy

A well-articulated investment policy serves multiple functions beyond guiding internal decisions. It signals to potential clients what the firm believes and how it operates. A policy that emphasizes evidence-based investing, global diversification, and disciplined risk management will attract clients who value those principles, just as a policy that emphasizes active management and concentrated portfolios will draw a different clientele. The policy thus helps match the firm with clients whose expectations align with its approach.

Transparent policies also build trust by setting clear expectations. Clients who understand the firm’s investment philosophy are less likely to be surprised by market volatility or disappointed by short-term performance. They recognize that returns will fluctuate and that the firm’s approach is designed for long-term outcomes rather than short-term gains. This clarity supports the durable relationships that benefit both clients and advisors.

Finally, a well-documented policy demonstrates regulatory compliance and provides a defensible framework for investment decisions. It establishes that the firm operates according to a defined process rather than ad hoc judgment, protecting both the firm and its clients by ensuring that decisions can be explained and justified by reference to a documented set of principles and procedures.

5.3 Client-Specific Investment Policy Statement

With the firm-level investment policy established, we now turn to the central task of this chapter: crafting a client-specific Investment Policy Statement. The firm-level policy provides the philosophical foundation and strategic framework. The client-specific IPS applies that framework to the unique circumstances of the clients, translating everything we have learned through the client analysis process into an actionable document that will guide every investment decision made on their behalf.

An Investment Policy Statement is a formal written document that outlines the investment objectives, constraints, and strategies for managing a specific client’s portfolio. It serves as the reference point for all investment decisions, ensuring that every trade, every allocation adjustment, and every rebalancing decision aligns with the client’s expressed wishes and the advisor’s professional judgment.

The IPS performs multiple functions simultaneously. It is a roadmap, charting the course from the client’s current financial position to their desired destination. It is an agreement, documenting the understanding between client and advisor about how the portfolio will be managed and what each party can expect from the relationship. It is a behavioral guardrail, providing discipline when emotions threaten to derail the long-term plan. And it is a living document, evolving as the client’s circumstances change and as markets deliver their inevitable surprises.

5.3.1 Structure of an Investment Policy Statement

While IPS documents vary across firms and clients, most follow a structure that ensures all essential elements are addressed. A comprehensive IPS typically includes the following sections.

Section 1: Client Identification and Background records basic identifying information about the client and provides a brief summary of their personal and financial circumstances. It captures the key facts that emerged from the client analysis process, including life stage, source of wealth, and any unique circumstances that will shape the investment strategy.

Section 2: Investment Objectives articulates the client’s financial goals in specific, measurable terms. Rather than vague aspirations, the IPS states concrete objectives with defined dollar amounts, time horizons, and priority levels. Each objective should be accompanied by any relevant assumptions about inflation or taxes that affect the required funding.

Section 3: Investment Constraints documents the factors that limit or constrain the investment strategy. These typically include time horizon, which may differ across goals; liquidity requirements, specifying the need for ready access to cash; tax considerations, including any strategies for tax-efficient investing; legal and regulatory constraints that affect investment choices; and any unique circumstances that bear on investment decisions, such as behavioral biases or emotional attachments to specific assets.

Section 4: Risk Tolerance captures the client’s willingness and ability to bear risk, drawing on the quantitative and qualitative assessments conducted during client analysis. It distinguishes between the client’s objective capacity for risk and their subjective comfort with volatility, and it specifies how any mismatches between ability and willingness will be resolved.

Section 5: Asset Allocation and Investment Strategy establishes the principles and governance framework that will guide asset allocation decisions. It does not specify the actual allocation percentages, since those are determined through a separate strategic asset allocation process and documented in a companion Investment Strategy Summary. Instead, this section focuses on the rules and guidelines that govern how allocation decisions are made and maintained.

Several key elements sit within this framework. Asset allocation principles articulate the core beliefs that will guide decisions, such as diversification, goal segmentation, and respect for risk constraints. Portfolio segmentation describes how the portfolio will be divided into distinct components aligned with each goal’s time horizon and liquidity requirements. Permissible asset classes define the types of investments that may be used in portfolio construction. The rebalancing policy specifies the conditions under which the portfolio will be rebalanced to maintain target allocations, including review frequency, tolerance bands, and methods for implementation. Investment selection criteria provide guidelines for evaluating and selecting individual securities or managers, consistent with the firm-level investment policy.

The specific strategic asset allocation targets, including the actual percentages allocated to each asset class, are determined through the process described in a later chapter and documented in a separate Investment Strategy Summary that accompanies the IPS. Keeping the targets separate from the IPS ensures that the IPS remains a stable governance document while the allocation can evolve with changing market conditions and client circumstances.

Section 6: Monitoring and Reporting specifies how the portfolio’s performance will be evaluated and how progress will be communicated to the client. It covers appropriate performance benchmarks for each asset class and for the portfolio as a whole, the frequency of formal portfolio reviews, and the content and format of reports provided to the client.

Section 7: Responsibilities clarifies the roles and duties of all parties involved in the investment process. Client responsibilities include providing accurate information, communicating changes in circumstances, and adhering to the agreed-upon plan. Advisor responsibilities include managing the portfolio in accordance with the IPS, providing timely reporting, and communicating material developments. The section may also address the responsibilities of custodians and other service providers.

Section 8: Review and Amendment Procedures establishes how the IPS will be maintained as a living document. It specifies the circumstances under which the IPS should be reviewed, the process for making amendments, and the requirement that any changes to the investment strategy be agreed upon by both parties and reflected in an updated IPS.

5.3.2 The IPS as a Behavioral Guardrail

One of the most important functions of the IPS is its role as a behavioral guardrail. As we learned in the previous chapter, even sophisticated clients are subject to cognitive and emotional biases that can lead to poor investment decisions. Loss aversion causes investors to sell during downturns. Overconfidence leads to excessive trading and inadequate diversification. Endowment bias prevents rational decisions about inherited assets.

A well-crafted IPS anticipates these biases and builds in safeguards. For a client prone to loss aversion, the IPS can specify that rebalancing occurs according to a predetermined schedule rather than in response to market movements. It can require that major changes to the investment strategy be discussed during a scheduled review rather than made impulsively. For a client with endowment bias, the IPS can explicitly acknowledge the attachment to certain assets and establish a plan for gradual diversification over time, perhaps committing to sell a fixed percentage each year regardless of market conditions. For a client with overconfidence, the IPS can clearly separate speculative holdings from conservative goal funding, establishing different investment policies for different purposes.

By documenting the agreed-upon strategy in advance, the IPS creates a commitment device that helps clients stay the course when emotions run high. When markets decline and the urge to sell arises, the client can refer to the IPS and recall that this volatility was anticipated and that the plan calls for remaining invested. When a difficult decision about a legacy holding must be made, the client can see that the decision is part of a thoughtful, long-term strategy rather than an impulsive act.

5.3.3 The IPS as a Living Document

An Investment Policy Statement is not static. It evolves as the client’s circumstances change, and most IPS documents specify a formal review schedule, typically annually, during which the advisor and client assess whether the investment strategy remains appropriate. These scheduled reviews provide a natural opportunity to evaluate portfolio performance against benchmarks and to discuss any adjustments that may be needed in light of changing conditions.

Not all reviews can wait for the annual cycle, however. Certain events should prompt an immediate reassessment of the IPS because they alter the foundation on which the original strategy was built. Significant changes in the client’s personal circumstances fall into this category. Marriage, divorce, the birth of a child, the death of a spouse, or a major health event can fundamentally reshape financial priorities and time horizons. Financial circumstances carry the same weight. An inheritance, a business sale, a career change, or a large unexpected expense shifts the resources available to fund the client’s goals and may change the return requirement or the risk that can be tolerated. External forces matter as well. A significant shift in market conditions or the economic outlook may justify a strategic adjustment to the portfolio. Changes in tax laws or regulations can alter the relative attractiveness of different asset classes or investment vehicles. Even a shift in the client’s own psychology, such as a material change in risk tolerance revealed by their reaction to a market event, should trigger a fresh conversation about whether the current strategy still fits.

When any of these events occur, the advisor and client return to the IPS together. They determine whether adjustments are needed and document any changes in a revised version of the document. This ongoing cycle of review and revision ensures that the IPS remains aligned with the client’s current situation and that both parties maintain a shared understanding of the investment strategy over the full course of their relationship.

5.4 Drafting an Investment Policy Statement for Mr. Miller

The framework above can now be applied to Mr. Miller. The aim is to turn a short client profile into an IPS that an advisor can use when making and reviewing investment decisions.

Mr. Miller’s age, location, source of wealth, net worth, investment preference, and allocation ranges are known. His spending needs, tax position, retained IPO shares, liquidity requirements, and reaction to previous losses are not. The IPS should record those gaps rather than quietly filling them with assumptions.

5.4.1 Section 1: Client Identification and Background

The identification and background section establishes the context for every later decision. It records that Mr. Miller is 39, lives in New York, works as a fintech entrepreneur, and recently saw his net worth rise to approximately USD 11 million after his company’s IPO.

His wealth was created through entrepreneurial effort. Some of it may still be tied to the fintech business. The advisor therefore needs to find out whether Mr. Miller still owns shares in the former company, whether those shares are subject to selling restrictions, and how much of his wealth and future income depends on technology and equity markets.

This matters because a portfolio can look diversified by security while remaining concentrated in the same economic risk as the client’s business.

5.4.2 Section 2: Investment Objectives

Mr. Miller’s primary objective is long-term capital growth with meaningful diversification. His age, mid-career position, substantial wealth, and distance from retirement support a long investment horizon. They also suggest that he can accept substantial short-term volatility.

That conclusion still needs to be tested against his spending needs and his actual response to losses. The advisor should also collect information about future commitments, income after the IPO, taxes, and any desired legacy. Until then, a qualitative objective is more defensible than a numerical return target: pursue long-term capital appreciation within the approved constraints. Returns are uncertain.

5.4.3 Section 3: Investment Constraints

The constraints section turns the simulation’s allocation rules into enforceable portfolio boundaries. The investable portfolio must satisfy all four ranges below, and the allocations must sum to 100 percent.

Asset Class Minimum Allocation Maximum Allocation
Government bonds 7% 35%
Corporate bonds 5% 20%
Equities 30% 100%
Alternatives 0% 15%

These are limits, not a recommendation to use every maximum. The maximums cannot all be reached at once because the total must equal 100 percent. The final strategic allocation belongs in the accompanying Investment Strategy Summary. It should follow an assessment of Mr. Miller’s total wealth, retained IPO exposure, liquidity needs, tax position, and response to risk.

Several facts remain to be collected before implementation: near-term spending and other commitments, the liquidity and selling restrictions attached to IPO shares, the tax treatment of the portfolio and IPO shares, and any legal or regulatory limits relevant to the advisory agreement.

Illiquid investments should not be selected until these questions have been answered.

5.4.4 Section 4: Risk Tolerance

Mr. Miller’s ability to take risk is high. He is 39, has substantial wealth, remains in mid-career, and has a long investment horizon. His willingness also appears high: he is eager to pursue new opportunities and comfortable with volatility in pursuit of growth.

That does not settle the portfolio design. As an Individualist, Mr. Miller may rely heavily on his own judgment, trade frequently, or concentrate in opportunities that resemble his business experience. The IPS can accommodate his growth objective while putting guardrails around those tendencies through diversification limits, stress testing, monitoring of technology exposure across his balance sheet, and a predetermined rebalancing process.

5.4.5 Section 5: Asset Allocation and Investment Strategy

Mr. Miller recognizes the importance of ESG considerations, but his primary objective is maximizing returns. ESG is therefore a selection preference, not an exclusion rule. It may inform investment choices when the opportunity remains compatible with the growth objective and the required diversification.

The case does not establish a negative screen, a positive screen, or a mandatory ESG mandate, so the IPS should not invent one. Any ESG-oriented choice should be evaluated for its effect on expected return, risk, fees, liquidity, and tracking error. Unless Mr. Miller gives further instructions, ESG considerations remain subordinate to the allocation ranges and the primary objective of long-term growth. The strategy explains how the portfolio will be managed; it does not need to contain final percentages, which belong in the Investment Strategy Summary. Its main principles are diversification across asset classes and regions, attention to total-wealth exposure including the former fintech company, cost awareness, liquidity management, and disciplined rebalancing.

The portfolio should be reviewed at least quarterly, with rebalancing when an asset-class weight moves outside its approved range, subject to tax and transaction costs. New contributions and cash flows can often correct smaller deviations, while a material change in Mr. Miller’s circumstances may require a broader strategic review. Each permitted investment should be assessed by its role in the portfolio: equities should contribute to long-term growth without simply repeating the risks of Mr. Miller’s business; government and corporate bonds require attention to credit quality, duration, liquidity, and diversification; and alternatives belong in the portfolio only when their liquidity, fees, transparency, and diversification benefit justify their inclusion.

5.4.6 Section 6: Monitoring and Reporting

The monitoring section explains how performance and compliance will be assessed. A blended benchmark should reflect the approved strategic allocation, not an arbitrary market index. If the final weights have not yet been selected, the IPS can refer to the benchmark composition in the accompanying Investment Strategy Summary.

Reports should cover valuation, performance relative to the benchmark, asset-class weights, fees, transactions, concentration exposures, and breaches of the permitted ranges. Include the retained IPO shares and related technology exposure. An account-level report may otherwise conceal concentration in total wealth.

5.4.7 Section 7: Responsibilities

The responsibilities section turns the IPS into an agreement about conduct. Mr. Miller must provide complete and accurate information, disclose retained IPO shares and selling restrictions, explain spending and future commitments, and report changes in his business, income, tax position, or willingness to bear risk. An attractive opportunity does not automatically belong in the portfolio.

The advisor manages within the approved ranges, evaluates investments in the context of the whole portfolio, monitors concentration and liquidity, documents rebalancing decisions, and communicates material developments. The custodian safeguards assets, executes authorized trades, and maintains accurate records. These duties are more useful when written in operational terms than as a general promise to act prudently.

5.4.8 Section 8: Review and Amendment Procedures

Review the IPS at least annually and whenever a material change occurs. For Mr. Miller, triggers include a change in the fintech business or retained IPO position, the end of a selling restriction, a major change in net worth or income, new spending or liquidity needs, a change in tax circumstances, or a demonstrated change in his response to market losses.

Amendments require agreement between Mr. Miller and the advisor and should be recorded in a dated version of the IPS. The Investment Strategy Summary may change as allocations are reviewed, but changes to the governing objectives, constraints, or risk assessment belong in the IPS itself.
