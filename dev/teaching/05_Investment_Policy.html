5.1 Introduction

In the previous chapter we introduced a framework for understanding clients. We learned to analyze their stage of life, the source of their wealth, their behavioral biases, and their personality types. We applied this framework to the Schmidt-Miller family and uncovered Klaus’s loss-averse conservatism, Elke’s emotional connection to her inherited portfolio, and Lukas’s overconfident risk-taking. We identified their goals, quantified their liquidity needs, assessed their risk tolerance, and calculated their return requirements.

The analytical work is now complete. The task shifts from diagnosis to prescription. We must translate this understanding of the family’s financial needs into a concrete investment plan.

An Investment Policy Statement, or IPS, is the document that bridges the gap between client analysis and portfolio construction. It takes everything we have learned about the client and translates it into a clear, written framework that will guide every investment decision made on their behalf. The IPS shows where the portfolio is going and documents the agreement between advisor and client about how to get there.

By the end of this chapter, we will understand not only what an Investment Policy Statement contains but also how to craft one that reflects the client’s complete financial circumstances. We will see how the concepts of client analysis translate into practical, actionable documents.

5.2 Firm-Level Investment Policy

An Investment Policy Statement for an individual client does not exist in isolation. It is created within the context of the firm that will implement it, and every investment firm operates according to a set of beliefs, principles, and practices that shape how client portfolios are managed. These may be formally documented in a firm-level investment policy or embedded in the firm’s culture and decision-making processes. Regardless of the format, this firm-level framework establishes the boundaries within which all client-specific IPS documents are developed. It ensures consistency across client relationships and provides the philosophical foundation for the advice the firm delivers.

A firm-level investment policy describes the investment philosophy, strategic approach, and guiding principles that define how the firm manages client assets. While the specific content varies from firm to firm, most investment policies address several core elements.

5.2.1 Investment Philosophy

The investment philosophy expresses the firm’s beliefs about how financial markets work and how investment returns are generated. These beliefs are not abstract. They shape every subsequent decision about portfolio construction and management.

Consider a firm that believes markets are generally efficient. In its view, consistently outperforming through security selection is difficult, so the firm will favor passive investment strategies. Portfolios will be constructed using low-cost index funds and exchange-traded funds that capture broad market returns rather than attempting to beat them. A firm with the opposite conviction, one that believes skilled investors can identify mispriced securities and generate excess returns, will dedicate resources to active management. It will invest in manager research, due diligence, and ongoing monitoring of selected strategies. Between these poles sits a firm that believes certain factors, such as value, momentum, or quality, have historically delivered premium returns. Such a firm may construct portfolios that tilt systematically toward these characteristics.

Each of these philosophical positions carries direct implications for how client portfolios will be built. A clear philosophy ensures that investment decisions are made consistently across the firm and that clients understand the intellectual framework underpinning the management of their assets.

5.2.2 Asset Allocation Framework

The asset allocation framework provides guidelines for how client portfolios should be diversified across asset classes. This section identifies the asset classes the firm considers suitable for client portfolios, which typically include global equities, fixed income instruments across the maturity and credit spectrum, real estate, and in some cases commodities or alternative investments.

The framework may also establish strategic allocation ranges that guide how these asset classes are combined for clients with different risk profiles. A conservative client might receive an allocation weighted heavily toward high-quality fixed income with a modest equity component. An aggressive client might hold a portfolio dominated by equities with smaller allocations to diversifying assets. By defining these building blocks and allocation ranges upfront, the framework ensures that all client portfolios, regardless of their specific circumstances, are constructed from a consistent set of components and adhere to the firm’s view of prudent diversification.

5.2.3 Risk Management Principles

Every firm must maintain a clear approach to managing the risks inherent in investing, and the risk management section of the policy translates this responsibility into a coherent framework. It addresses how the firm identifies, measures, and mitigates the various forms of risk that client portfolios face, moving from broad categories to specific, actionable limits.

Diversification across asset classes and geographic regions is the primary defense against market risk, the possibility that broad market movements will erode portfolio value. By spreading exposures, the firm reduces the likelihood that a decline in any single market will inflict lasting damage on client portfolios. Credit risk, which arises when bond issuers default on their obligations, requires a different set of controls. The policy establishes minimum quality standards for fixed income holdings and caps the concentration of exposure to any single issuer, ensuring that no individual default can meaningfully impair a portfolio. Liquidity risk is addressed by restricting the proportion of client assets allocated to illiquid holdings and by maintaining adequate cash reserves. This protects against the scenario in which a client needs to raise cash but finds that assets cannot be sold without accepting a steep discount. Operational risk, rooted in failed processes, systems, or human error, is managed through documented procedures, segregation of duties, and regular testing of internal controls.

These broad principles are reinforced through specific requirements that make them enforceable. The policy sets explicit limits on exposure to any single security, sector, or issuer. It provides guidelines for the use of derivatives and other instruments that may be employed for hedging or risk reduction. It also establishes the procedures for ongoing risk monitoring and defines the protocols that govern how the firm responds when limits are breached or when market conditions shift materially. What begins as a statement of principle becomes, through these mechanisms, a practical operating framework for protecting client assets.

5.2.4 Ethical and ESG Considerations

Many firms now incorporate environmental, social, and governance considerations into their investment policies. Negative screening excludes companies or industries that conflict with the firm’s stated values. Positive screening actively seeks companies with strong ESG practices. Full integration considers ESG factors alongside traditional financial metrics in the investment analysis process. The policy may also address any firm-level commitments to responsible investing, such as adherence to established principles or participation in industry initiatives.

By defining its stance on these issues, the firm provides guidance for advisors who must address client questions about the alignment of their portfolios with personal values.

5.2.5 The Function of a Firm-Level Policy

A well-articulated investment policy serves multiple functions beyond guiding internal decisions. It signals to potential clients what the firm believes and how it operates. A policy that emphasizes evidence-based investing, global diversification, and disciplined risk management will attract clients who value those principles, just as a policy that emphasizes active management and concentrated portfolios will draw a different clientele. The policy thus helps match the firm with clients whose expectations align with its approach.

Transparent policies also build trust by setting clear expectations. Clients who understand the firm’s investment philosophy are less likely to be surprised by market volatility or disappointed by short-term performance. They recognize that returns will fluctuate and that the firm’s approach is designed for long-term outcomes rather than short-term gains. This clarity supports the durable relationships that benefit both clients and advisors.

Finally, a well-documented policy demonstrates regulatory compliance and provides a defensible framework for investment decisions. It establishes that the firm operates according to a defined process rather than ad hoc judgment, protecting both the firm and its clients by ensuring that decisions can be explained and justified by reference to a documented set of principles and procedures.

5.3 Client-Specific Investment Policy Statement

With the firm-level investment policy established, we now turn to the central task of this chapter: crafting a client-specific Investment Policy Statement. The firm-level policy provides the philosophical foundation and strategic framework. The client-specific IPS applies that framework to the unique circumstances of the individual or family, translating everything we have learned through the client analysis process into an actionable document that will guide every investment decision made on their behalf.

An Investment Policy Statement is a formal written document that outlines the investment objectives, constraints, and strategies for managing a specific client’s portfolio. It serves as the reference point for all investment decisions, ensuring that every trade, every allocation adjustment, and every rebalancing decision aligns with the client’s expressed wishes and the advisor’s professional judgment.

The IPS performs multiple functions simultaneously. It is a roadmap, charting the course from the client’s current financial position to their desired destination. It is an agreement, documenting the understanding between client and advisor about how the portfolio will be managed and what each party can expect from the relationship. It is a behavioral guardrail, providing discipline when emotions threaten to derail the long-term plan. And it is a living document, evolving as the client’s circumstances change and as markets deliver their inevitable surprises.

5.3.1 Structure of an Investment Policy Statement

While IPS documents vary across firms and clients, most follow a structure that ensures all essential elements are addressed. A comprehensive IPS typically includes the following sections.

Section 1: Client Identification and Background records basic identifying information about the client and provides a brief summary of their personal and financial circumstances. It captures the key facts that emerged from the client analysis process, including life stage, source of wealth, and any unique circumstances that will shape the investment strategy.

Section 2: Investment Objectives articulates the client’s financial goals in specific, measurable terms. Rather than vague aspirations, the IPS states concrete objectives with defined dollar amounts, time horizons, and priority levels. Each objective should be accompanied by any relevant assumptions about inflation or taxes that affect the required funding.

Section 3: Investment Constraints documents the factors that limit or constrain the investment strategy. These typically include time horizon, which may differ across goals; liquidity requirements, specifying the need for ready access to cash; tax considerations, including any strategies for tax-efficient investing; legal and regulatory constraints that affect investment choices; and any unique circumstances that bear on investment decisions, such as behavioral biases or emotional attachments to specific assets.

Section 4: Risk Tolerance captures the client’s willingness and ability to bear risk, drawing on the quantitative and qualitative assessments conducted during client analysis. It distinguishes between the client’s objective capacity for risk and their subjective comfort with volatility, and it specifies how any mismatches between ability and willingness will be resolved.

Section 5: Asset Allocation and Investment Strategy establishes the principles and governance framework that will guide asset allocation decisions. It does not specify the actual allocation percentages, since those are determined through a separate strategic asset allocation process and documented in a companion Investment Strategy Summary. Instead, this section focuses on the rules and guidelines that govern how allocation decisions are made and maintained.

Several key elements sit within this framework. Asset allocation principles articulate the core beliefs that will guide decisions, such as diversification, goal segmentation, and respect for risk constraints. Portfolio segmentation describes how the portfolio will be divided into distinct components aligned with each goal’s time horizon and liquidity requirements. Permissible asset classes define the types of investments that may be used in portfolio construction. The rebalancing policy specifies the conditions under which the portfolio will be rebalanced to maintain target allocations, including review frequency, tolerance bands, and methods for implementation. Investment selection criteria provide guidelines for evaluating and selecting individual securities or managers, consistent with the firm-level investment policy.

The specific strategic asset allocation targets, including the actual percentages allocated to each asset class, are determined through the process described in a later chapter and documented in a separate Investment Strategy Summary that accompanies the IPS. Keeping the targets separate from the IPS ensures that the IPS remains a stable governance document while the allocation can evolve with changing market conditions and client circumstances.

Section 6: Monitoring and Reporting specifies how the portfolio’s performance will be evaluated and how progress will be communicated to the client. It covers appropriate performance benchmarks for each asset class and for the portfolio as a whole, the frequency of formal portfolio reviews, and the content and format of reports provided to the client.

Section 7: Responsibilities clarifies the roles and duties of all parties involved in the investment process. Client responsibilities include providing accurate information, communicating changes in circumstances, and adhering to the agreed-upon plan. Advisor responsibilities include managing the portfolio in accordance with the IPS, providing timely reporting, and communicating material developments. The section may also address the responsibilities of custodians and other service providers.

Section 8: Review and Amendment Procedures establishes how the IPS will be maintained as a living document. It specifies the circumstances under which the IPS should be reviewed, the process for making amendments, and the requirement that any changes to the investment strategy be agreed upon by both parties and reflected in an updated IPS.

5.3.2 The IPS as a Behavioral Guardrail

One of the most important functions of the IPS is its role as a behavioral guardrail. As we learned in the previous chapter, even sophisticated clients are subject to cognitive and emotional biases that can lead to poor investment decisions. Loss aversion causes investors to sell during downturns. Overconfidence leads to excessive trading and inadequate diversification. Endowment bias prevents rational decisions about inherited assets.

A well-crafted IPS anticipates these biases and builds in safeguards. For a client prone to loss aversion, the IPS can specify that rebalancing occurs according to a predetermined schedule rather than in response to market movements. It can require that major changes to the investment strategy be discussed during a scheduled review rather than made impulsively. For a client with endowment bias, the IPS can explicitly acknowledge the attachment to certain assets and establish a plan for gradual diversification over time, perhaps committing to sell a fixed percentage each year regardless of market conditions. For a client with overconfidence, the IPS can clearly separate speculative holdings from conservative goal funding, establishing different investment policies for different purposes.

By documenting the agreed-upon strategy in advance, the IPS creates a commitment device that helps clients stay the course when emotions run high. When markets decline and the urge to sell arises, the client can refer to the IPS and recall that this volatility was anticipated and that the plan calls for remaining invested. When a difficult decision about a legacy holding must be made, the client can see that the decision is part of a thoughtful, long-term strategy rather than an impulsive act.

5.3.3 The IPS as a Living Document

An Investment Policy Statement is not static. It evolves as the client’s circumstances change, and most IPS documents specify a formal review schedule, typically annually, during which the advisor and client assess whether the investment strategy remains appropriate. These scheduled reviews provide a natural opportunity to evaluate portfolio performance against benchmarks and to discuss any adjustments that may be needed in light of changing conditions.

Not all reviews can wait for the annual cycle, however. Certain events should prompt an immediate reassessment of the IPS because they alter the foundation on which the original strategy was built. Significant changes in the client’s personal circumstances fall into this category. Marriage, divorce, the birth of a child, the death of a spouse, or a major health event can fundamentally reshape financial priorities and time horizons. Financial circumstances carry the same weight. An inheritance, a business sale, a career change, or a large unexpected expense shifts the resources available to fund the client’s goals and may change the return requirement or the risk that can be tolerated. External forces matter as well. A significant shift in market conditions or the economic outlook may justify a strategic adjustment to the portfolio. Changes in tax laws or regulations can alter the relative attractiveness of different asset classes or investment vehicles. Even a shift in the client’s own psychology, such as a material change in risk tolerance revealed by their reaction to a market event, should trigger a fresh conversation about whether the current strategy still fits.

When any of these events occur, the advisor and client return to the IPS together. They determine whether adjustments are needed and document any changes in a revised version of the document. This ongoing cycle of review and revision ensures that the IPS remains aligned with the client’s current situation and that both parties maintain a shared understanding of the investment strategy over the full course of their relationship.

5.4 Drafting the Schmidt-Miller Investment Policy Statement

With a clear understanding of the IPS structure and purpose, we now begin the practical work of drafting a comprehensive Investment Policy Statement for the Schmidt-Miller family. This section focuses on the foundational elements of any IPS: a clear statement of the client’s investment objectives and the constraints within which the portfolio must be managed. These elements flow directly from the client analysis conducted in the previous chapter and must be documented with precision to ensure that all subsequent investment decisions align with the family’s circumstances.

5.4.1 Client Identification and Background

Every IPS begins with a clear identification of the client and a brief summary of their relevant personal and financial circumstances. The section serves as a reference for the advisor and as documentation that a thorough inquiry into the client’s situation has been conducted.

For the Schmidt-Miller family, the identification section reads as follows.

Primary Clients: Klaus Schmidt-Miller, age 68, and Elke Schmidt-Miller, age 65.

Related Party: Lukas Schmidt-Miller, age 28, son.

Klaus Schmidt-Miller is an orthopedic surgeon who has practiced for over three decades. He is in the process of selling his stake in the surgical practice he co-founded, a transaction that will result in after-tax proceeds of approximately €18 million within the next sixty days, and he intends to retire fully upon completion of this sale. Elke Schmidt-Miller retired two years ago from her career as a high school principal. She receives an inflation-indexed pension of €45,000 annually and recently inherited a diversified portfolio of stocks and bonds from her parents, valued at €2.5 million. The inherited portfolio has a low cost basis, creating significant unrealized capital gains that will influence any decision to reposition those assets. Lukas Schmidt-Miller, their son, is a software engineer residing in Berlin. He holds a 5 percent equity stake in his startup employer, currently valued at €50,000 for tax purposes but highly speculative. Lukas is financially independent from his parents and manages his own affairs, though he expects to receive financial support from them for a future home down payment.

The family resides in a stable Central European jurisdiction with a progressive income tax system. The top marginal rate on ordinary income is 40 percent, and realized capital gains are taxed at a flat 20 percent rate. Tax-advantaged retirement accounts exist within the local framework. The family has no debt and owns their primary residence free and clear, valued at €2.2 million.

5.4.2 Investment Objectives

The investment objectives section translates the family’s goals into specific, measurable targets that will guide portfolio construction and performance evaluation. Each objective is clearly defined, quantified, and assigned a priority level so that when trade-offs must be made, the advisor knows which objectives take precedence. Client analysis has identified five distinct objectives for the Schmidt-Miller family, spanning immediate cash flow needs through multi-decade legacy goals.

The family’s most pressing need is retirement income. The portfolio must generate €160,000 in annual after-tax income, indexed for inflation, to fund Klaus and Elke’s retirement lifestyle. Funded from the core portfolio of approximately €18.55 million, this objective is immediate and ongoing with a time horizon exceeding thirty years, and it is non-negotiable. All other goals are subordinate to this requirement. Next in priority, Elke’s art gallery requires €500,000 within approximately twelve months to establish a gallery in Lucerne. While this project matters deeply to her, it remains discretionary and could be delayed if circumstances require. The funds will be set aside from the core portfolio and held in instruments that preserve capital with near certainty.

Lukas’s down payment introduces a medium-term need of €400,000 within three to five years to assist with the purchase of a home in Berlin. Both the amount and the timing carry some flexibility, which broadens the range of suitable investment approaches for this capital. The Lake Garda retreat, calling for €2 million within five years to acquire and renovate a vacation property, is similarly discretionary, and the purchase date can be adjusted if markets perform poorly. This flexibility will inform how the funds are invested during the accumulation period. Finally, the family seeks legacy preservation. After all other goals have been funded, the remaining portfolio must preserve its real value over a horizon exceeding thirty years to transfer wealth to Lukas and future grandchildren. This objective anchors the longest time horizon in the plan and provides the strongest case for growth-oriented investments.

The retirement income objective is stated after tax, so the portfolio must generate sufficient pre-tax returns to meet this requirement given the family’s tax situation. All monetary amounts are stated in current euros, and future values will be adjusted for inflation as documented in the IPS review process.

5.4.3 Investment Constraints

The investment constraints section documents the factors that limit or shape the investment strategy. These constraints arise from the client’s personal circumstances, the legal and regulatory environment, and the firm’s policies. Together with the investment objectives, they define the boundaries within which the portfolio must be constructed.

Multiple time horizons coexist within the Schmidt-Miller household, each corresponding to a different goal. The retirement income objective has a horizon exceeding thirty years, which would ordinarily allow for growth-oriented investments in the core portfolio. This must be balanced against Klaus’s low risk tolerance. At the other extreme, the art gallery objective has a one-year horizon requiring near-complete capital preservation, and these funds cannot be exposed to equity market risk. Between these poles sit the down payment objective, with a three-to-five-year horizon that allows modest risk but demands increasing conservatism as the target date approaches, and the Lake Garda objective, with a five-year horizon and some flexibility to delay the purchase if markets perform poorly. The legacy objective stretches beyond thirty years, permitting a growth orientation in the residual portfolio after other goals have been funded.

Liquidity needs are equally varied and pressing. Annual retirement withdrawals of €160,000 require that the first year’s spending be held in cash or very short-term instruments. The €500,000 for the art gallery is needed in approximately twelve months. The €400,000 for Lukas’s down payment may be needed within three years, and the €2 million for the Lake Garda property falls due in five years. These requirements mean the portfolio cannot be invested as a single block. Funds must be segmented into distinct components, each with its own investment approach aligned to its time horizon and liquidity need.

Tax considerations add further complexity. Klaus’s €18 million sale proceeds are after-tax, so no embedded capital gain liability exists for these funds. Elke’s inherited portfolio of €2.5 million, by contrast, has a low cost basis and substantial unrealized capital gains that would trigger tax at the 20 percent rate upon sale. Annual portfolio income will be taxed at progressive rates up to 40 percent, making the tax efficiency of different asset classes and structures a material concern. Klaus’s retirement account of €800,000 grows tax-free in the interim, though withdrawals will be taxed as ordinary income. These factors will influence asset location decisions, the timing of any sales from Elke’s inherited portfolio, and the selection of tax-efficient investment vehicles.

The family is subject to the tax and regulatory requirements of their jurisdiction. Lukas’s residence in Berlin introduces cross-border considerations for any gifts or transfers, which should be reviewed with tax specialists. All investments must comply with applicable securities laws and regulations.

The client analysis also revealed several unique circumstances that constrain the investment strategy. Klaus’s loss aversion is the most significant. He views the €18 million as the fruit of a lifetime of labor and is averse to losses, so any strategy that exposes significant principal to market volatility will cause him anxiety and may lead to poor decisions during downturns. The portfolio must be structured to minimize the frequency and severity of losses, even at the cost of lower expected returns.

Elke’s endowment bias creates a different kind of constraint. Her strong emotional connection to the inherited portfolio makes her reluctant to sell those assets, even when diversification would be prudent. The investment strategy must accommodate this attachment while still building a diversified overall portfolio, which may involve keeping a core holding of inherited stocks while diversifying around them. Lukas’s overconfidence and separate goals require compartmentalization. His speculative startup equity is separate from the family’s core wealth and should be treated as such, while his down payment funds must be managed conservatively despite his personal risk tolerance. Finally, Elke’s art gallery is a passion project rather than a financial investment. The €500,000 allocated to this goal should be treated as an expense with an expected return of zero, not as part of the investment portfolio seeking growth.

5.4.4 Risk Tolerance Assessment

The risk tolerance section documents both the client’s ability to take risk and their willingness to do so. Where these two dimensions diverge, the section specifies how the mismatch will be resolved.

Klaus Schmidt-Miller presents a clear divergence. His ability to take risk is high, grounded in a long time horizon exceeding thirty years, substantial wealth, no debt, and flexible discretionary goals. His willingness to take risk, however, is low. Extreme loss aversion rooted in his view of wealth as hard-won, a history of conservative financial decisions, and an expressed discomfort with market volatility all point toward a conservative posture. In this case, willingness governs. The portfolio will be managed according to Klaus’s low risk tolerance, and this becomes the binding constraint for all investment decisions affecting the joint portfolio.

Elke Schmidt-Miller’s profile is more balanced. Her ability to take risk is moderate: she has a long horizon but is already in retirement, though her pension provides baseline income security. Her willingness to take risk is also moderate. She is open to complexity but constrained by the emotional attachment she holds toward her inherited assets. Her moderate tolerance will be accommodated within the framework set by Klaus’s more conservative boundary, with specific provisions for the inherited holdings.

Lukas Schmidt-Miller’s personal wealth sits at the opposite end of the spectrum. Both his ability and willingness to take risk are high, supported by a forty-year horizon, an early-career position with developing human capital, and a temperament comfortable with volatility. He views his startup equity as a speculative opportunity and expresses confidence in his ability to recover from losses. His personal wealth will be managed according to this aggressive profile. The down payment funds, however, are a different matter. Despite Lukas’s personal risk tolerance, the short three-to-five-year horizon and the commitment of these funds to a specific purchase mean the ability to take risk is low. Lukas agrees these funds must be preserved, and they will be managed conservatively.

Synthesizing these individual profiles, the core family portfolio funding Klaus and Elke’s retirement and legacy goals will be managed according to Klaus’s low risk tolerance as the binding constraint. Elke’s moderate tolerance and endowment bias will be accommodated within this framework. Lukas’s separate goals will be managed independently, with the down payment funds in a conservative strategy and his personal wealth following an aggressive profile.

5.4.5 Return Requirements

After setting aside funds for the specific shorter-term goals, approximately €18.55 million remains for the long-term retirement and legacy portfolio. This capital must generate €160,000 in annual after-tax income, indexed for inflation, for Klaus and Elke’s lifetime, while also preserving the real value of the remaining principal for legacy transfer to Lukas and future generations.

A simple spending rate calculation, dividing €160,000 by €18.55 million, yields 0.86 percent. This figure understates the true return requirement because it ignores both inflation and taxes. Assuming 2.5 percent average inflation, the withdrawal needed in year ten rises to approximately €205,000 simply to maintain purchasing power. Taxes further increase the required pre-tax return, since withdrawals and portfolio income will be taxed at rates up to 40 percent. To deliver €160,000 after tax, the portfolio must earn considerably more before tax, and to maintain the real value of the principal, returns must at least keep pace with inflation after both withdrawals and taxes.

A complete analysis incorporating these factors indicates that the portfolio needs to earn a real after-tax return of approximately 3.5 to 4.5 percent to sustainably fund the desired spending while preserving the real value of the legacy. This translates to a nominal pre-tax return target in the range of 6 to 8 percent, depending on inflation and the tax efficiency of portfolio construction.

The funds allocated to specific shorter-term goals carry different return requirements. The €500,000 for the art gallery has a one-year horizon and requires near-zero return, with capital preservation as the paramount concern. These funds will be held in cash or very short-term bonds. The €400,000 for the down payment has a three-to-five-year horizon and requires a modest return with preservation priority. These funds will be held in a short-term bond ladder or conservative balanced fund. The €2 million for the Lake Garda property has a five-year horizon and requires a moderate return. Some growth is needed to offset inflation, but preservation remains important, and these funds will be held in a medium-term bond ladder with a modest equity allocation if flexibility exists in the purchase date.

A nominal pre-tax return target of 6 to 8 percent for the core portfolio is realistic but not guaranteed. Historical returns on a diversified portfolio of global equities and bonds have averaged in this range over long periods, yet this average masks significant variability. A portfolio constructed to achieve the target will likely carry an equity allocation of 40 to 60 percent, implying potential peak-to-trough losses of 20 to 30 percent in severe bear markets. This level of volatility sits at the upper limit of what Klaus’s loss aversion can tolerate.

The portfolio must therefore be structured to minimize the frequency and severity of losses while still pursuing the required return. Achieving this balance may require a higher allocation to high-quality bonds than a purely mathematical optimization would suggest, the use of strategies that provide downside protection, and a dedicated income component that offers psychological comfort even if it is not mathematically optimal.

5.4.6 Asset Allocation and Investment Strategy Framework

With the client’s objectives, constraints, risk tolerance, and return requirements documented, we now establish the governance framework that will guide asset allocation and investment decisions. The actual allocation percentages are determined through the strategic asset allocation process and documented in a separate Investment Strategy Summary. What follows are the principles, boundaries, and procedures that will govern how those allocation decisions are made and maintained over time.

The Schmidt-Miller portfolio will be constructed according to six core principles.

Diversification requires spreading the portfolio across multiple asset classes to reduce reliance on any single source of return and to manage overall volatility. Different asset classes perform differently under varying economic conditions, and a balanced portfolio can achieve more consistent returns than any single asset class alone.

Goal segmentation requires allocating funds to distinct sub-portfolios, each aligned with the time horizon and liquidity requirements of a specific financial goal. Short-term goals will be funded with capital preservation instruments, while long-term goals may include growth-oriented assets. This segmentation ensures that near-term needs never compromise the long-term strategy.

Respect for the binding constraint means that Klaus’s low risk tolerance governs the core retirement portfolio. All allocation decisions will respect this boundary even if it implies lower expected returns. The portfolio will be structured to minimize the frequency and severity of losses to ensure that Klaus can adhere to the plan through market cycles.

Tax efficiency requires that asset location decisions be made with careful attention to tax consequences. Tax-inefficient assets such as taxable bonds will be prioritized for Klaus’s retirement account, while tax-efficient assets such as equities will be prioritized for taxable accounts. Sales from Elke’s inherited portfolio will be managed over time to control tax impact.

Cost awareness means that all investment decisions will consider the impact of costs on net returns. Where appropriate, low-cost passive vehicles such as index funds and exchange-traded funds will be used to gain core market exposure. Active strategies will be considered only in areas where research suggests the potential for excess returns sufficient to justify higher fees.

Behavioral accommodation requires that the portfolio be structured to address the biases identified during client analysis, as detailed in the unique circumstances section.

Based on the family’s multiple goals with different time horizons, the portfolio will be divided into four segments. The liquidity bucket will fund the art gallery with a one-year horizon, using capital preservation instruments such as cash or very short-term bonds. The first medium-term bucket will fund Lukas’s down payment with a three-to-five-year horizon, using a conservative approach that becomes increasingly conservative as the target date approaches. The second medium-term bucket will fund the Lake Garda property with a five-year horizon, using a moderate approach that allows some growth potential while maintaining preservation as the priority. The long-term bucket will fund retirement income and legacy with a horizon exceeding thirty years, using a growth-oriented approach constrained by Klaus’s low risk tolerance. The specific amounts allocated to each bucket and the detailed investment approach will be documented in the Investment Strategy Summary that accompanies this IPS.

The asset classes considered suitable for the Schmidt-Miller portfolio include developed market equities across Swiss, European, North American, and Asia-Pacific markets; emerging market equities in selected markets with appropriate risk controls; government bonds from Switzerland and developed markets; investment-grade corporate bonds diversified across issuers and sectors; inflation-linked bonds for inflation protection; real estate investment trusts for diversification and income; and cash and cash equivalents including money market funds, short-term government bonds, and bank deposits. Private equity and hedge funds are excluded due to liquidity constraints and complexity. Cryptocurrencies and other speculative digital assets are excluded, as are structured products with opaque fee structures or inappropriate risk characteristics.

The portfolio will be rebalanced quarterly, with rebalancing triggered when any asset class deviates from its target allocation by more than five percentage points. Rebalancing will be accomplished by directing new cash flows to underweight asset classes and, when necessary, selling overweight positions. All rebalancing decisions will consider tax implications, with a preference for using new contributions and dividend reinvestments to adjust allocations in taxable accounts.

Investment selection criteria vary by vehicle type. For passive investments such as index funds and exchange-traded funds, the criteria include low expense ratio relative to category peers, tracking error consistent with the stated index, sufficient assets under management and trading liquidity, and provider reputation and operational stability. For active investments, the criteria include demonstrated performance relative to benchmark over a full market cycle, a consistent and well-understood investment process, a stable and experienced investment team, fees that are reasonable given the strategy, and alignment of interests between manager and investors. For fixed income investments, the criteria include credit quality appropriate to the role in the portfolio, duration consistent with the strategic allocation, and, for individual bonds, diversification across issuers and sectors.

For Elke’s inherited holdings, a core portion of the most emotionally significant stocks may be maintained, subject to a maximum concentration limit of 5 percent of total portfolio value and regular review to ensure continued suitability. The remaining inherited assets will be gradually diversified over time according to a schedule documented in the Investment Strategy Summary. This accommodation respects Elke’s endowment bias while ensuring that the overall portfolio moves steadily toward a fully diversified structure.

Tax Considerations

Unique Circumstances and Behavioral Considerations

Klaus’s loss aversion is the dominant behavioral constraint. He views the €18 million as the fruit of a lifetime of labor and is averse to losses. Any strategy that exposes significant principal to market volatility will cause him anxiety and may lead to poor decisions during downturns. The portfolio must therefore be structured to minimize the frequency and severity of losses.

Elke’s endowment bias means she feels a strong emotional connection to her inherited portfolio and is reluctant to sell these assets. The investment strategy must accommodate this attachment while still building a diversified overall portfolio. Lukas’s overconfidence and separate goals require compartmentalization. His speculative startup equity is separate from the family’s core wealth, and his down payment funds must be managed conservatively despite his personal risk tolerance. Finally, Elke’s art gallery is a passion project, not a financial investment. The €500,000 allocated to this goal should be treated as an expense with an expected return of zero.
