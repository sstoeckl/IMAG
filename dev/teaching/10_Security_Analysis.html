10.1 10.1 Introduction: The Selection Effect

The preceding chapters moved from the client’s circumstances to the strategic asset allocation. We established the policy mix. We examined tactical tilts. We addressed the constraints of implementation. What remains is the final step: selecting the specific securities that will populate each asset class allocation.

Security analysis is the process of evaluating individual investments for their suitability. It asks not whether equities as a class are attractive, but whether a particular stock, bond, or fund merits the capital it would receive. The strategic allocation provides the architecture. Security analysis provides the materials. A sound allocation can be undermined by poor security selection, just as a well-designed building can be compromised by faulty materials.

This distinction connects directly to the Brinson-Fachler attribution framework from Chapter 9. Recall that the model decomposes active return into allocation effects and selection effects. The allocation effect captures whether overweighting or underweighting an asset class added value. The selection effect captures whether the specific securities chosen within that asset class beat its benchmark. Security analysis feeds the selection effect. It is the engine that drives the \(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i}\) term in the Brinson-Fachler formula. A positive selection effect means the securities chosen outperformed the asset class benchmark. Security analysis is the discipline that seeks to make that happen.

At its core, security analysis rests on a simple distinction: intrinsic value versus market price. Intrinsic value is what an asset is worth, based on its expected future cash flows, its risk, and its growth prospects. Market price is what the market is willing to pay for it today. The two are not always equal. Markets are mostly efficient, as Chapter 8 discussed, but they are not perfectly efficient. Sentiment, liquidity, and institutional constraints can push prices away from intrinsic value. Security analysis seeks to identify those gaps, to buy assets trading below their intrinsic value and to avoid or sell those trading above it.

The approach taken depends on the asset class. For equities, fundamental analysis examines financial statements, competitive position, and management quality. For bonds, credit analysis assesses the issuer’s ability to meet its obligations. For funds and ETFs, the analysis shifts to the manager’s process, the fee structure, and the tracking error relative to the benchmark. Each asset class requires its own analytical framework, but the underlying question is the same: is this security priced in a way that compensates us adequately for the risks we are taking?

This chapter surveys the methods of security analysis. We begin with the macroeconomic context, then examine fundamental equity analysis, fixed income analysis, and quantitative screening approaches. The treatment is not exhaustive—entire textbooks are devoted to each method—but it provides the framework within which security selection decisions are made. The chapter concludes by populating the Schmidt-Miller portfolio with specific securities, translating the strategic weights from Chapter 7 into a concrete set of holdings.

10.2 10.2 Fundamental Equity Analysis: The Bottom-Up Approach

Fundamental equity analysis seeks to determine the intrinsic value of a company by examining its financial statements, its competitive position, and its prospects for future growth. It is a bottom-up approach. It begins with the company itself, not with the economy or the sector, though both provide context for the assessment.

Financial statement analysis forms the foundation. The three primary statements—the income statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement—tell the story of a business in numbers. Revenue growth reveals whether the company is expanding or stagnating. Profit margins reveal its pricing power and cost discipline. Return on equity reveals how efficiently it deploys shareholder capital. But the raw numbers are only a starting point. The analyst must assess their quality.

Quality of earnings refers to the sustainability and reliability of reported profits. A company that consistently meets or beats earnings estimates may be managing earnings through aggressive revenue recognition, changes in inventory accounting, or the timing of expenses. Cash flow conversion provides a check. If reported earnings are strong but operating cash flow is weak, the earnings may be of low quality. The cash flow statement strips away non-cash items and reveals how much actual cash the business generates. A high ratio of operating cash flow to net income suggests that earnings are backed by real cash. A low ratio suggests the opposite.

Capital structure analysis examines how the company finances its operations. The mix of debt and equity affects both risk and return. High leverage amplifies returns when business conditions are favorable but increases the risk of financial distress when they deteriorate. The analyst assesses whether the company’s debt load is sustainable given its cash flow, the maturity profile of its obligations, and the covenants that govern its borrowing.

Valuation translates the analysis of the business into an estimate of what its shares are worth. Two broad approaches exist. Absolute valuation models estimate intrinsic value directly, based on the present value of expected future cash flows. Relative valuation models compare the company’s pricing multiples to those of similar firms. Both approaches require a discount rate, and the choice of discount rate depends on which cash flows are being discounted.

The distinction between the cost of equity and the weighted average cost of capital is essential. When valuing equity directly, by discounting free cash flow to equity, the appropriate discount rate is the cost of equity, derived from the CAPM or a multi-factor model. When valuing the entire enterprise, by discounting free cash flow to the firm, the appropriate discount rate is the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC. The WACC blends the cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt, weighted by their proportions in the company’s capital structure:

\[ \text{WACC} = \frac{E}{E + D} \cdot r_e + \frac{D}{E + D} \cdot r_d \cdot (1 - t) \]

Where \(E\) is the market value of equity, \(D\) is the market value of debt, \(r_e\) is the cost of equity, \(r_d\) is the cost of debt, and \(t\) is the corporate tax rate. The tax shield on debt reduces the effective cost of borrowing, reflecting the deductibility of interest payments in most jurisdictions. A company with significant debt financing will have a WACC below its cost of equity, all else equal.

The discounted cash flow model, or DCF, can be applied to either equity or the enterprise. When applied to equity, it projects the free cash flows available to shareholders after all expenses, taxes, and debt payments, then discounts them at the cost of equity:

\[ V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{FCFE_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} + \frac{TV}{(1 + r_e)^n} \]

When applied to the enterprise, it projects the free cash flows available to all capital providers—both equity holders and debt holders—and discounts them at the WACC. The resulting enterprise value is then reduced by net debt to arrive at equity value. The terminal value, regardless of which approach is used, typically accounts for more than half of the total valuation. Small changes in the long-term growth rate or the terminal multiple produce large changes in the estimated intrinsic value.

The residual income model offers an alternative. It values equity directly as the sum of current book value and the present value of future residual income—the earnings that exceed the required return on equity. This approach is particularly useful for financial companies, where book value is a meaningful metric, and for companies that do not generate positive free cash flow.

Relative valuation sidesteps the complexity of forecasting cash flows. It values a company by comparing its pricing multiples to those of comparable firms. The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the most common. A company trading at a lower P/E than its peers may be undervalued, or it may deserve a lower multiple because of slower growth, higher risk, or poorer management. Enterprise value to EBITDA, or EV/EBITDA, is popular precisely because it uses the enterprise value, reflecting the claims of both equity and debt holders, and a measure of operating profit before depreciation and amortization. This neutrality toward capital structure makes it easier to compare companies with different levels of debt. Price-to-book, or P/B, is used for asset-heavy industries like banking and insurance. Each multiple has strengths and weaknesses. The skilled analyst uses several in combination, looking for consistency across metrics rather than relying on any single one.

Qualitative factors are harder to measure but no less important. A company’s competitive position—its moat—determines whether it can sustain its profits against rivals. Porter’s Five Forces framework assesses the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of suppliers and customers, the threat of substitute products, and the intensity of rivalry within the industry. A wide moat, built on brand strength, switching costs, network effects, or cost advantages, protects profitability and justifies a higher valuation.

Management quality matters. A skilled management team allocates capital wisely, invests in projects that earn returns above the cost of capital, and communicates honestly with shareholders. A poor management team destroys value through ill-considered acquisitions, excessive compensation, or strategic missteps. Assessing management requires reading earnings call transcripts, shareholder letters, and the track record of capital allocation decisions over time. The cost of capital is not merely a mechanical input to a DCF model. It is the hurdle rate against which management’s investment decisions should be judged. A management team that consistently earns returns above the WACC creates value. One that earns returns below it destroys value, regardless of how rapidly earnings are growing.

Scalability of the business model determines the pace at which growth can be achieved. A software company with a product that can be distributed globally at near-zero marginal cost can grow far faster than a manufacturing company that must build new factories to increase output. The market size, the competitive landscape, and the company’s ability to execute all factor into the growth assumptions that drive the valuation.

Fundamental equity analysis ends with a decision. If the estimated intrinsic value exceeds the market price by a sufficient margin of safety, the stock is a candidate for purchase. If the market price exceeds intrinsic value, it is a candidate for sale or avoidance. The margin of safety protects against errors in the analysis. No valuation is precise. The analyst who demands a discount to intrinsic value before buying builds a cushion against the unknown. That discipline is at the heart of fundamental investing.

10.3 10.3 Quantitative Equity Analysis: Multi-Factor Models

Fundamental analysis examines one company at a time. Quantitative analysis examines thousands. It uses statistical models to identify characteristics that explain differences in returns across securities. Where the fundamental analyst reads annual reports, the quantitative analyst runs regressions.

The shift began with the recognition that the Capital Asset Pricing Model, introduced in Chapter 6, provides an incomplete description of equity returns. Beta captures sensitivity to the market portfolio, but it does not capture everything. Stocks with similar betas can have very different returns if they differ on other dimensions. This observation gave rise to Arbitrage Pricing Theory and, from there, to the multi-factor models that now dominate quantitative equity analysis.

The Fama-French framework is the cornerstone. It begins with the market factor from the CAPM and adds two additional factors: size and value. The size factor, called SMB for Small Minus Big, captures the historical tendency of smaller companies to outperform larger ones. The value factor, called HML for High Minus Low, captures the tendency of companies with high book-to-market ratios—value stocks—to outperform those with low ratios—growth stocks.

The three-factor model expresses expected return as:

\[ E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i [E(R_m) - R_f] + s_i \cdot \text{SMB} + h_i \cdot \text{HML} \]

Where \(s_i\) is the stock’s sensitivity to the size factor and \(h_i\) is its sensitivity to the value factor. A small-cap value stock would have positive loadings on both SMB and HML. A large-cap growth stock would have negative loadings on both.

The three-factor model was a significant improvement over the CAPM. It explained a larger portion of the cross-sectional variation in equity returns. But it was not complete. Subsequent research identified additional factors. The momentum factor, WML for Winners Minus Losers, captures the tendency of stocks that have performed well in the recent past to continue performing well in the near term. The quality factor captures the tendency of companies with strong profitability, low leverage, and stable earnings to deliver higher risk-adjusted returns.

A five-factor model incorporating market, size, value, profitability, and investment has become the standard in academic research. In practice, different managers emphasize different factors based on their investment philosophy and the evidence they find most compelling.

Factor tilting translates this framework into portfolio construction. Each stock in the universe is assigned a score on each factor. The scores are typically expressed as Z-scores, measuring how many standard deviations the stock’s characteristic lies from the cross-sectional mean. A stock with a high book-to-market ratio, a low market capitalization, and strong recent momentum would score positively on value, size, and momentum. A stock with the opposite characteristics would score negatively.

The quantitative analyst combines these scores into a composite signal. The weights assigned to each factor reflect the analyst’s conviction and the empirical evidence on factor efficacy. The composite signal ranks stocks from most attractive to least attractive. The portfolio overweights the former and underweights or excludes the latter.

Factor tilting is not without challenges. Factor premiums vary over time. A value tilt that underperforms for years tests the conviction of the manager and the patience of the client. Momentum can reverse violently when market conditions change. The factors themselves can become crowded, as the growing popularity of factor-based strategies compresses the premiums they seek to capture.

These challenges do not invalidate the approach. They counsel humility in its application. Factor tilts should be modest, diversified across multiple factors, and evaluated over full market cycles. The quantitative analyst who expects every factor to work every year will be disappointed. The analyst who understands that factor premiums are compensation for bearing systematic risk—and that risk sometimes shows up—will be better prepared for the inevitable periods of underperformance.

The rise of multi-factor investing has blurred the line between active and passive management. A portfolio that tilts systematically toward value, size, and quality is not actively managed in the traditional sense. It does not rely on stock-specific research or market timing. But neither is it passive, in the sense of simply tracking a market-cap-weighted index. It occupies a middle ground, sometimes called smart beta or factor investing. For the Schmidt-Miller portfolio, this approach offers a way to pursue incremental returns within the equity allocation without the higher costs and concentration risk of traditional active management.

10.4 10.4 Fixed Income Analysis: Government and Corporate Bonds

Equity analysis seeks to estimate future cash flows and the discount rate at which they should be valued. Fixed income analysis, by contrast, deals largely in contractual cash flows. The coupon payments and principal repayment are specified in advance, at least for bonds free of default risk. This certainty shifts the analytical focus from forecasting growth to measuring risk. Interest rate risk and credit risk dominate the fixed income landscape.

The return on a bond comes from three sources. The first is the yield, the coupon income earned on the bond’s face value. For a government bond held to maturity with no default, the yield to maturity at the time of purchase is the primary driver of return. The second source is rolldown. As a bond ages and its maturity shortens, its yield typically declines along the yield curve. The bond’s price rises as it rolls down the curve, adding to total return even if the level of interest rates remains unchanged. The third source is the change in spreads, relevant for bonds that carry credit risk. When a bond’s credit spread narrows, its price rises relative to otherwise similar government bonds. When spreads widen, the price falls.

These components decompose the return into what is earned passively, through the passage of time and the receipt of coupons, and what is earned actively, through changes in the interest rate and credit environment.

Interest rate risk is measured by duration and convexity. Duration captures the sensitivity of a bond’s price to a small change in its yield. Modified duration provides an approximation of the percentage price change for a one percentage point change in yield. A bond with a modified duration of 7 would be expected to rise approximately 7 percent if yields fell by one percentage point, or fall by the same amount if yields rose.

Convexity refines this estimate. Duration assumes a linear relationship between price and yield, but the true relationship is curved. Convexity measures the degree of that curvature. For large changes in yield, the duration-only estimate becomes inaccurate, and convexity must be added to the calculation.

\[ \%\Delta P \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2 \]

Where \(D_{\text{mod}}\) is the modified duration, \(C\) is the convexity, and \(\Delta y\) is the change in yield. Convexity is always positive for standard bonds, meaning that a bond’s price rises more when yields fall than it declines when yields rise by the same amount. This asymmetry is valuable. All else equal, investors prefer bonds with higher convexity.

For corporate bonds and sovereign bonds of less creditworthy issuers, credit analysis joins interest rate analysis. The framework rests on the traditional Four Cs of credit. Capacity refers to the issuer’s ability to generate sufficient cash flow to meet its obligations. It is assessed through financial ratios—interest coverage, debt to EBITDA, free cash flow to debt—and through an understanding of the issuer’s business model and competitive position. Collateral refers to the assets available to secure the debt in the event of default. Secured bonds backed by specific assets offer greater recovery than unsecured bonds that rely only on the general credit of the issuer.

Covenants are the contractual protections embedded in the bond agreement. They may restrict the issuer from taking on additional debt, paying dividends, or selling assets without creditor consent. Strong covenants protect bondholders. Weak covenants leave them exposed. Character refers to the willingness of management to honor its obligations, a qualitative assessment based on the issuer’s track record, its communication with creditors, and the alignment of management’s incentives with those of bondholders.

Spread analysis evaluates whether a bond offers adequate compensation for the risks identified through the Four Cs. The credit spread is the difference between the bond’s yield and the yield of a comparable government bond. It reflects expected default losses and a risk premium for bearing uncertainty around those losses. A bond trading at a spread wider than its peers, after accounting for differences in credit quality, duration, and liquidity, may be cheap. A bond trading at a narrower spread may be rich.

Spread analysis often begins with the benchmark curve, typically the government bond yield curve for the currency in which the bond is denominated. A corporate bond’s yield is decomposed into the risk-free rate at the relevant maturity plus a spread. That spread can be further decomposed into the expected loss component and the risk premium component. The analyst judges whether the spread is sufficient given the credit profile of the issuer and the prevailing level of spreads in the market. Spreads that are tight by historical standards suggest that investors are being poorly compensated for credit risk. Spreads that are wide suggest attractive compensation, though they may also signal elevated default risk that justifies the wider spread.

Fixed income analysis, whether applied to government bonds or corporate credit, is fundamentally an exercise in assessing risk and return at the security level. The analysis does not ask whether bonds as an asset class belong in the portfolio—that is the strategic allocation decision. It asks which specific bonds best fulfill the role that the strategic allocation has assigned to them. For a government bond allocation designed to provide safety and liquidity, the emphasis falls on duration management and the issuer’s creditworthiness. For a corporate bond allocation designed to enhance yield, the emphasis shifts to credit selection and spread analysis. The tools described in this section serve both purposes, adapted to the specific objectives of each allocation.

10.5 10.5 Analyzing Alternative Assets: Commodities and Cryptocurrencies

Equities generate cash flows. Bonds pay coupons. Commodities and cryptocurrencies do neither. They are alternative assets, distinct in structure and in the analytical frameworks required to evaluate them. Their inclusion in a portfolio is not mandatory. The Schmidt-Miller IPS excludes cryptocurrencies and limits commodities to a tactical role. But the analyst must understand these assets, if only to explain why they are excluded.

Commodity analysis begins with supply and demand. The price of oil, copper, or wheat is determined by the balance between production and consumption. When demand outstrips supply, prices rise. When supply exceeds demand, prices fall. The analyst tracks inventory levels, production forecasts, and consumption trends to gauge the direction of that balance. Geopolitical shocks—a pipeline disruption, a trade embargo, a war in a producing region—can shift supply abruptly. Weather can destroy crops. Technological change can unlock new sources of supply or reduce demand for existing ones.

The cost of carry links spot prices to futures prices. It includes storage costs, insurance, and the interest cost of financing the commodity position. When the futures price exceeds the spot price, the market is in contango. When the spot price exceeds the futures price, it is in backwardation. The shape of the futures curve determines the roll yield, the return earned by rolling a futures contract forward as it approaches expiration.

In a contango market, rolling a long position incurs a cost. The expiring contract is sold at a lower price, and the new contract is purchased at a higher one. The roll yield is negative. In a backwardated market, the roll yield is positive. The expiring contract is sold at a higher price than the new contract is purchased. Over time, the roll yield can dominate the total return from commodity exposure, outweighing the change in the spot price. The commodity analyst must understand the futures curve. Ignoring it is not an option.

Cryptocurrency analysis operates on entirely different principles. There are no cash flows, no physical supply constraints in the traditional sense, and no central authority managing supply. The analysis focuses on network fundamentals. Bitcoin and Ethereum are networks, and their value depends on adoption, usage, and security.

Metcalfe’s Law provides a framework. It states that the value of a network is proportional to the square of the number of its users. More users create more connections, and more connections create more value. Active addresses on the blockchain serve as a proxy for users. Transaction volumes and frequency provide additional metrics. A network with growing adoption and increasing usage has a plausible claim to increasing value. A network with stagnant or declining usage faces headwinds.

Hash rate measures the computational power securing proof-of-work networks like Bitcoin. A higher hash rate indicates greater security, as more computing power would be required to attack the network. It also reflects miner confidence, as miners invest in equipment and electricity based on their expectation of future prices. Declining hash rates can signal miner capitulation and network stress.

Tokenomics refers to the economic design of a cryptocurrency. Supply is governed by protocol rules, not by central bank committees. Bitcoin’s supply is capped at twenty-one million coins, with the rate of new issuance halving every four years. This halving cycle creates predictable supply shocks that have historically preceded price appreciation. Other cryptocurrencies employ different mechanisms: burning tokens to reduce supply, distributing staking rewards to incentivize network participation, or adjusting issuance algorithmically based on network conditions. The analyst must understand the rules governing supply. They are as fundamental to cryptocurrency valuation as earnings growth is to equity valuation.

On-chain analysis exploits the transparency of public blockchains. Every transaction is recorded on a distributed ledger, visible to anyone. Analysts track the movements of large holders, known as whales, whose transactions can signal impending selling pressure or accumulation. Exchange flows—the movement of coins between wallets and exchanges—provide insight into investor behavior. Large inflows to exchanges suggest that holders are preparing to sell. Large outflows suggest accumulation and a preference for self-custody. These signals are noisy, but they provide a window into market dynamics that is unavailable in traditional asset classes.

The inclusion of alternative assets in a portfolio is a strategic decision. The analytical frameworks described here inform that decision. For the Schmidt-Miller family, commodities may play a limited tactical role, as discussed in Chapter 8. Cryptocurrencies are excluded from the policy portfolio due to their volatility, their uncertain regulatory status, and their incompatibility with Klaus’s loss aversion. The analyst understands them regardless, because understanding what is excluded is as important as understanding what is included. The boundaries of the permissible investment universe are not arbitrary. They are the product of analysis, grounded in the characteristics of the assets and the circumstances of the client.

10.6 10.6 Sustainability Analysis: ESG as a Risk/Return Overlay

The preceding sections treated security analysis as a purely financial exercise. Cash flows, discount rates, and valuation multiples drove the assessment. A growing body of evidence suggests that non-financial factors—environmental, social, and governance characteristics—also affect risk and return. Sustainability analysis integrates these factors into the security selection process.

ESG analysis is not a single method. It spans a spectrum from exclusionary screening to full integration. Exclusionary screening removes companies or entire sectors that conflict with the investor’s values. Tobacco, controversial weapons, and thermal coal are common exclusions. Integration, by contrast, treats ESG factors as inputs to the investment process, alongside traditional financial metrics. A company with strong environmental practices, good labor relations, and a well-structured board may warrant a higher valuation or a lower risk premium than a peer with weaker characteristics.

The rationale is twofold. First, strong ESG practices may serve as a proxy for quality. A company that manages its environmental footprint carefully, treats its employees fairly, and maintains robust board oversight is likely well-managed in other respects. The discipline required to excel on ESG dimensions correlates with the discipline required to allocate capital efficiently and execute strategy effectively. Second, weak ESG practices expose a company to risks that financial statements may not yet reflect. An oil company with a poor environmental record faces potential regulatory penalties, cleanup costs, and reputational damage. A bank with weak governance may be vulnerable to fraud or excessive risk-taking. These risks can remain dormant for years and then materialize suddenly, destroying shareholder value.

The data challenge complicates ESG analysis. Unlike financial statements, which follow standardized accounting principles, ESG reporting lacks uniformity. Companies disclose different metrics, use different methodologies, and report with varying degrees of rigor. Third-party rating agencies attempt to fill the gap, but their ratings often diverge. A company rated highly by one provider may score poorly on another. The lack of standardization creates noise and opens the door to greenwashing—the practice of making misleading claims about environmental credentials to attract capital or enhance reputation.

Greenwashing takes many forms. A company may announce ambitious emissions targets without a credible plan to achieve them. It may highlight its investments in renewable energy while ignoring the far larger emissions from its core operations. It may publish glossy sustainability reports while lobbying against environmental regulation. The analyst must distinguish between genuine commitment and cosmetic disclosure. This requires going beyond the ratings to examine the underlying data, the track record of follow-through, and the alignment between the company’s stated values and its actions.

Materiality focuses the analysis. Not every ESG factor matters for every sector. Environmental factors are material for energy companies, miners, and heavy manufacturers, where emissions, water usage, and waste disposal directly affect costs and regulatory exposure. Social factors are material for consumer-facing companies, where labor practices, supply chain conditions, and product safety shape brand reputation and customer loyalty. Governance factors are material across all sectors but are particularly important for banks and financial institutions, where risk management, board independence, and executive compensation structures determine the resilience of the franchise.

The Sustainability Accounting Standards Board and similar bodies have developed sector-specific materiality maps that identify the ESG issues most likely to affect financial performance in each industry. These maps provide a starting point. The analyst uses them to focus attention where it matters most, avoiding the trap of treating all ESG factors as equally relevant for all companies.

Integrating ESG into security analysis does not require abandoning traditional financial metrics. It requires supplementing them. A discounted cash flow model remains the core of equity valuation. The ESG overlay adjusts the inputs—reducing the growth rate for a company facing regulatory headwinds, increasing the discount rate for a company with governance concerns, or extending the competitive advantage period for a company with strong stakeholder relationships. The adjustment is judgmental. It is not formulaic. But it brings the analysis closer to the full reality of the business.

For the Schmidt-Miller portfolio, ESG integration is not a binding constraint. The family has expressed no specific values-based exclusions. But the quality dimension of ESG aligns with Klaus’s preference for stability and downside protection. Companies with strong governance and prudent risk management are less likely to experience the sudden collapses that trigger his loss aversion. The ESG lens, applied as a quality overlay rather than an exclusionary screen, serves the family’s objectives without limiting the investable universe. It is not about ethics. It is about identifying risks that the financial statements have not yet priced.

10.7 10.7 Security Selection in a Portfolio Context

Security analysis ends with a list of candidates. Portfolio construction begins with that list and asks a different set of questions. Not every undervalued stock belongs in the portfolio. Not every attractively priced bond is suitable. The transition from analysis to selection requires weighing conviction against concentration, opportunity against capacity.

Active share measures how much a portfolio differs from its benchmark. It is calculated as the sum of absolute active weights divided by two:

\[ \text{Active Share} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |w_{p,i} - w_{b,i}| \]

Where \(w_{p,i}\) is the portfolio weight of security \(i\) and \(w_{b,i}\) is its weight in the benchmark. An active share of zero means the portfolio mirrors the benchmark exactly. An active share of one hundred percent means it holds nothing in common with the benchmark. High active share implies high conviction. The manager is betting that the selected securities will outperform the index by a margin sufficient to justify the deviation.

Active share alone does not capture risk. Two portfolios can have identical active shares but very different risk profiles. One might be diversified across many sectors with modest stock-specific tilts. The other might be concentrated in a single sector with large stock-specific bets. The tracking error, introduced in Chapter 9, captures the risk dimension:

\[ \sigma_{\text{active}} = \sqrt{w_a^T \Sigma w_a} \]

Where \(w_a\) is the vector of active weights and \(\Sigma\) is the covariance matrix of security returns. A high active share combined with low tracking error suggests many small, uncorrelated bets. A high active share with high tracking error suggests a few concentrated bets. The desired combination depends on the manager’s skill and the breadth of opportunities, as the Fundamental Law of Active Management established:

\[ IR = IC \times \sqrt{BR} \]

Conviction and concentration travel together. A manager who identifies ten stocks as deeply undervalued and allocates capital equally among them is expressing higher conviction—and accepting greater concentration risk—than one who holds two hundred names with modest active weights. The former portfolio will deviate significantly from the benchmark. If the analysis is correct, the returns will be exceptional. If the analysis is wrong, the losses will be equally so.

The trade-off can be formalized through the expected excess return and its variance. Suppose the manager has an expected alpha of \(\alpha_i\) for each security, with an expected tracking error of \(\sigma_{\alpha,i}\). The portfolio’s expected alpha is the weighted sum of individual alphas. Its variance depends on the covariance of those alphas and the number of independent positions:

\[ \sigma_{\alpha,p}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{a,i}^2 \sigma_{\alpha,i}^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \neq i} w_{a,i} w_{a,j} \rho_{ij} \sigma_{\alpha,i} \sigma_{\alpha,j} \]

Greater diversification reduces the portfolio-level tracking error, but at the cost of diluting the expected alpha per unit of active risk. The optimal number of positions balances these forces.

Closet indexing arises when active share is low and tracking error is minimal. The manager charges active fees but delivers benchmark-like returns. The investor pays for conviction but receives a portfolio that hugs the index. This failure mode has a mathematical footprint. The active share is below sixty percent. The tracking error is below two percent. The R-squared with the benchmark exceeds 0.95. These metrics, taken together, are a diagnostic for a portfolio that is active in name only.

Capacity and liquidity impose further constraints. Every security has a maximum position size beyond which trading becomes difficult. The market impact of a trade can be modeled as a function of the trade size relative to average daily volume:

\[ \text{Impact Cost} \approx \sigma_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{\frac{Q}{\text{ADV}}} \]

Where \(Q\) is the quantity traded and \(ADV\) is the average daily volume. The larger the trade relative to normal market activity, the greater the price movement against the trader. A small-cap stock with limited volume cannot absorb a large order without significant impact. The manager who identifies an opportunity in such a stock must either accept a smaller position than conviction warrants or incur higher costs that reduce the net alpha.

Liquidity constraints are particularly binding for large portfolios. The Schmidt-Miller long-term portfolio, at over €18 million, is not large by institutional standards, but position sizing still requires attention. An allocation of 2 percent is €360,000. In a liquid large-cap stock, this is easily absorbed. In a smaller company, it may represent several days of average volume. The resulting implementation shortfall, modeled in Chapter 9, would consume a material portion of the expected excess return.

Security selection is the point at which analysis meets action. Candidates are identified through fundamental or quantitative analysis. Their contribution to the portfolio’s expected excess return and tracking error is evaluated. Positions are sized to reflect conviction while respecting concentration limits, liquidity constraints, and the risk budget. The process is iterative and quantitative, blending judgment with the discipline that the mathematical framework imposes. The final portfolio is not simply a collection of good ideas. It is a deliberate combination of securities, each chosen because its expected contribution to alpha exceeds its marginal contribution to risk.

10.8 10.8 Case Study: Selecting the Schmidt-Miller Securities

The strategic asset allocation from Chapter 7 established the policy weights. The long-term portfolio holds 35 percent global equities, 15 percent Swiss equities, 30 percent corporate bonds, and 20 percent government bonds. These weights are abstract. They must be translated into specific securities, selected according to the analytical frameworks developed in this chapter. The challenge is not simply to find a fund or a bond that fits each category. It is to select securities that serve the family’s distinct circumstances—Klaus’s loss aversion, Elke’s emotional attachment to her inheritance, and the need for reliable income.

The equity allocation requires two distinct solutions. The global equity component, at 35 percent of the long-term portfolio, demands broad diversification and low cost. A global quality ETF, screening for companies with high profitability, low leverage, and stable earnings, serves this purpose. Quality companies tend to decline less in bear markets, a characteristic that aligns with Klaus’s binding constraint on downside risk. The screening criteria include return on equity above 15 percent, debt-to-equity below 50 percent, and positive earnings growth over the past five years. Companies that fail these screens are excluded, regardless of their valuation. The result is a portfolio tilted toward businesses with durable competitive advantages and conservative financial management.

The Swiss equity component presents a more complex problem. Elke already holds €2.5 million in inherited Swiss equities with a low cost basis. Selling these positions outright would trigger a 20 percent capital gains tax, consuming €500,000 of value. The solution is to treat the inherited holdings as a Swiss equity allocation and supplement them with a Swiss value fund that tilts toward high-dividend, low-volatility stocks. The inherited stocks are concentrated in a few names—likely the large-cap industrials and financials that dominated the Swiss market a generation ago. The value fund adds diversification across sectors and introduces a systematic, factor-based approach that the inherited portfolio lacks.

The inherited holdings are mapped against the fund’s holdings. Overlapping positions are retained in the inherited account to avoid realizing gains. Non-overlapping positions are added through the fund. Over time, as the inherited stocks appreciate further or as tax-loss harvesting opportunities arise, the portfolio migrates toward the factor-based fund. The transition is gradual. Elke’s emotional attachment is respected. No stock is sold without her understanding and consent. But the direction of travel is clear: from a concentrated, legacy portfolio to a diversified, systematically managed one.

Fixed income selection serves a different purpose. The government bond allocation is not designed to generate return. It is designed to immunize the family’s commitment to €160,000 in annual retirement spending. The approach is liability-driven. A ladder of high-quality CHF-denominated bonds is constructed, with maturities staggered to match the expected withdrawals over the next ten years. Each bond is held to maturity, eliminating interest rate risk for that portion of the portfolio. The credit quality is impeccable. Swiss Confederation bonds and obligations of Swiss cantons with the highest credit ratings form the core.

The yield on these bonds is low, consistent with the capital market assumptions from Chapter 6. A ten-year Swiss government bond yields approximately 1.2 percent. The ladder produces an average yield slightly higher, reflecting the inclusion of slightly longer maturities and the modest credit spread of cantonal bonds. The expected return is not exciting. It is not meant to be. These bonds exist so that Klaus can sleep at night, knowing that the family’s spending needs are met regardless of what happens in equity markets.

The corporate bond allocation seeks a higher yield while maintaining investment-grade quality. The selection criteria emphasize issuer diversification, duration management, and credit quality. Bonds are selected from a universe of Swiss and European corporate issuers with ratings of BBB or higher. The average duration is held below five years to limit interest rate sensitivity. The portfolio avoids sectors with elevated ESG risk—thermal coal, controversial weapons, tobacco—not as an ethical screen but because these sectors face uncertain regulatory futures that could impair credit quality.

The question of commodities or gold arises from the tactical discussion in Chapter 8. The strategic allocation does not include commodities. But the Investment Clock framework and the stagflation scenario suggest that a small allocation to an inflation-hedging asset may reduce shortfall risk without materially increasing volatility. Gold, rather than a broad commodity ETF, is the preferred vehicle. Gold’s negative correlation with real interest rates and its role as a safe haven during periods of market stress complement the portfolio’s existing exposures. A 2 percent allocation, funded from the government bond allocation, adds diversification without consuming significant tracking error.

Bitcoin is considered and rejected. The network fundamentals are compelling. Metcalfe’s Law suggests that value scales with adoption. On-chain metrics show growing usage. But volatility exceeds 60 percent per year. Drawdowns of 80 percent or more are common. For a family whose binding constraint is Klaus’s loss aversion, an asset with this risk profile is incompatible with the IPS. The analysis does not dismiss Bitcoin as worthless. It dismisses it as unsuitable for this particular client.

The final portfolio of securities reflects the layered logic of the entire investment process. The strategic allocation provides the architecture. The tactical analysis provides the tilt. Security selection provides the materials. Each layer is consistent with the layer above it. Each security is chosen not because it is the best in isolation but because it contributes something the portfolio needs—and because it does so without violating the constraints that define the Schmidt-Miller family’s relationship with their wealth.
